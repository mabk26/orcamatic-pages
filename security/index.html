What ORCA asks for, and what it does with it.

An automation extension gets a broad view of your browser by design, so it should be explicit about how it uses that. This page is the plain version: every permission, the reason it exists, where your data sits, and what never leaves the machine.

  • Workflows stored locally
  • No card data on our side
  • Disclosures published in the store

The permissions, one by one.

These are the permissions ORCA requests, each with what it enables and what it does not. Nothing here is hidden behind “to improve your experience”.

Manifest permissions as declared in the extension and in the Chrome Web Store listing.
PermissionWhat it lets ORCA doWhy it is needed, and where it stops
activeTabAct on the tab you are looking at, when you start a run or open the panel.Without it the extension cannot see the page you want to automate.
Host permission for the sites you automateRead and act inside the pages you open, so one install works on a portal, a CRM and an internal tool alike.Chrome cannot grant a browser automation tool one site at a time and keep it usable. Why the broad host permission exists.
scriptingInject the picker and the runner into a page so it can highlight a target, type a value or read a table.It runs while a workflow is building or executing, not continuously in the background.
storageKeep your workflow profiles, rules, extracted values and run logs on your own machine.This is the local repository; it is not a sync service and nothing here is mirrored to us.
alarmsWake the scheduler at the time you set for a recurring run.An alarm starts a saved workflow; it does not collect anything by itself.
offscreenKeep long runs and scheduled work alive while the service worker goes idle, as Manifest V3 requires.No extra page access: the document is a lifecycle mechanism, not a data collector.
webNavigationFollow the tab as a workflow moves between pages, so a step can wait for the next page to be ready.Used to time steps and to notice a redirect; not to build a browsing history.

Where the data sits.

Two columns, and the split is the whole design: automation state on your machine, cloud calls only when you choose an AI step.

On your machine

  • Workflow profiles and saved rules, including CSS selectors, XPath locators and element labels
  • Schedule definitions and run parameters
  • Extracted values while a run is going: table grids, structured data blocks, your own variables
  • Run and diagnostics logs: timestamps, attempt counts, step status
  • Your pacing preferences, rate-limit intervals and verification-pause settings

It leaves your device only if you export a profile or share a workflow yourself.

Sent to ORCA Cloud — only during an AI step

  • One viewport snapshot, when you deliberately start an AI planning step
  • A structural summary of the interactive elements on that page
  • The instruction you typed for that step

Processed in memory to decide the next action and returned. Not sold, rented or traded to brokers or advertisers, and not used to build a profile of you.

Rule-based and scheduled runs do not send page content anywhere, so they also do not draw on your monthly AI balance.

Declared to Google, stated here too.

The store requires publishers to declare the data an extension handles. ORCA’s declaration says it handles personally identifiable information, user activity and website content — because that is what filling a form and reading a page involves. We would rather you read that from us first.

It also carries the three limited-use promises: not sold to third parties outside approved use, not used for anything unrelated to the core function, and never used for creditworthiness or lending decisions.

  • Card numbers and banking credentials are handled by Lemon Squeezy, never stored by us
  • Password managers fill their own fields — never script a password field into a workflow
  • No global keyboard hook: ORCA acts inside the pages you automate, not in other applications
